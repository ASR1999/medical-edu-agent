A Stateful, Privacy-Preserving Medical Dialogue System via Retrieval-Augmented Generation and Domain-Adapted ASRAbstractThe escalating burden of clinical documentation contributes significantly to provider burnout, while parallel challenges in healthcare access limit patient engagement. This project addresses these dual issues by designing, implementing, and evaluating a novel, end-to-end medical dialogue system. The proposed architecture integrates four key components to solve critical research gaps: (1) a domain-adapted Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) module, fine-tuned using Quantized Low-Rank Adaptation (QLoRA) to enhance accuracy on specialized medical terminology; (2) an on-premise, privacy-preserving Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) pipeline utilizing the ChromaDB vector store to ensure Patient Health Information (PHI) security; (3) a stateful dialogue manager built with LangGraph to handle complex, cyclical clinical conversations; and (4) a Large Language Model (LLM) for reasoning and response generation.The system was evaluated using a multi-faceted methodology. The ASR module's performance was measured against both standard Word Error Rate (WER) and the clinically vital Medical Concept Word Error Rate (MC-WER). The QLoRA-tuned ASR model demonstrated a significant reduction in MC-WER, confirming its superior ability to transcribe critical medical terms accurately. The end-to-end QA system was benchmarked against standard datasets, including PubMedQA, achieving performance competitive with established models. A rigorous adversarial "Red Teaming" evaluation was conducted to assess system safety, revealing a 15% overall failure rate and highlighting specific risks related to factual hallucinations and potential harm.This report concludes that the proposed architecture is a feasible and practical solution for resource-constrained environments, successfully balancing performance, privacy, and computational cost. However, the safety analysis, guided by the ethical principle of non-maleficence, indicates that the system is not yet suitable for autonomous clinical deployment. It establishes a viable path toward a "human-in-the-loop" clinical co-pilot and provides a robust ethical framework for future development.Chapter 1: Introduction1.1 The Crisis in Clinical Documentation and Information AccessThe healthcare sector is grappling with a profound, dual-natured crisis of information management and access. Firstly, healthcare providers are subjected to an immense burden of clinical documentation and administrative tasks, which are critical for patient care, billing, and legal records. This documentation overhead frequently leads to physician burnout, reduced time for direct patient interaction, and decreased overall satisfaction among clinicians.1 AI-powered systems present a significant opportunity to alleviate this strain by automating tasks such as data entry and patient scheduling, thereby freeing healthcare professionals to focus on high-value clinical care.2Secondly, a parallel crisis exists in patient access to information. In numerous developing nations, as well as in rural and remote regions of developed countries, populations lack sufficient access to healthcare professionals and proper infrastructure.2 This gap can be partially filled by advancements in telemedicine, mHealth, and conversational AI, which use mobile technologies to provide initial diagnoses, triage, and medical consultations, particularly for underserved populations.21.2 The Emergence of Conversational AI in HealthcareIn response to these challenges, conversational AI has emerged as a transformative technology. This paradigm is built upon two foundational pillars: Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) and Large Language Models (LLMs).ASR systems are designed to form the "ears" of a clinical AI, capturing the spoken interactions between a patient and provider and converting them into precise, written text records.4 This transcription is the essential first step for any downstream automation. Following transcription, LLMs act as the "brain," leveraging their training on massive text datasets to understand, interpret, contextualize, and generate human-like language.5 The integration of these technologies promises a future where clinical notes are generated automatically, and patients can access reliable medical information through conversational interfaces.1.3 Problem Statement and Research GapDespite their promise, existing AI solutions in healthcare are hindered by four fundamental limitations, which define the research gap this project aims to address:ASR Inaccuracy: General-purpose ASR systems, trained on non-medical data, fail catastrophically when confronted with specialized medical terminology, diverse accents, and noisy clinical environments.4 These errors are not trivial; they include misinterpreted drug names, incorrect dosages, and anatomical confusions, posing a direct threat to patient safety.4LLM Unreliability: Even state-of-the-art, domain-specialized LLMs (such as Med-PaLM and ChatDoctor) are known to "hallucinate"—producing factually incorrect but plausible-sounding statements—and suffer from "outdated knowledge," as their internal knowledge is static to their last training date.6Privacy and Security: The vast majority of high-performance AI models are cloud-based. This architectural model requires sensitive Patient Health Information (PHI) to be transmitted to third-party vendors, creating significant privacy, security, and regulatory compliance risks under frameworks like HIPAA.10Static Interaction: Many current medical "chatbots" are simple, single-turn question-and-answer systems. This fails to capture the reality of a clinical dialogue, which is inherently stateful, cyclical, and complex, often requiring context from earlier in the conversation.12While the literature often addresses these four challenges in isolation, the true research gap is the lack of a holistic, integrated system that addresses all four simultaneously. A project that only fine-tunes an LLM, for example, is still vulnerable to ASR errors and privacy risks. This project posits that a viable medical dialogue system must be domain-adapted (to solve ASR accuracy), reliable (to solve LLM hallucinations), privacy-preserving (to solve data security), and stateful (to solve the dialogue challenge).1.4 Thesis ContributionsThis M.Tech project makes three primary contributions to the field of applied clinical AI:System Architecture: The design and implementation of a novel, end-to-end medical conversational AI architecture. This system vertically integrates a Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT)-adapted ASR model, a stateful dialogue manager (LangGraph), and a privacy-preserving, on-premise Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) pipeline (ChromaDB).Comprehensive Evaluation: A multi-faceted evaluation of this integrated system using a purpose-built benchmark strategy. This includes domain-specific ASR metrics (Medical Concept Word Error Rate), standard medical QA benchmarks (PubMedQA), and a critical safety and ethics evaluation (Adversarial Red Teaming).Empirical Analysis: An empirical analysis of the performance-versus-resource trade-off of PEFT methods (LoRA vs. QLoRA) for fine-tuning ASR models in a resource-constrained (M.Tech project) environment, providing a practical pathway for future research.1.5 Report StructureThis report is structured as follows: Chapter 2 provides a comprehensive review of the foundations and related work in clinical ASR, medical LLMs, RAG, and PEFT. Chapter 3 details the specific system architecture and design, justifying the selection of each component. Chapter 4 presents the quantitative results of the experimental evaluation, including ASR performance, QA accuracy, and safety testing. Chapter 5 provides a critical discussion and interpretation of these results, analyzing their implications and limitations. Chapter 6 concludes the report with a summary of contributions and directions for future work.Chapter 2: Foundations and Related Work2.1 The Evolution of Clinical Speech RecognitionThe effective transcription of spoken language is the bedrock of any clinical dialogue system. However, the medical domain presents unique and high-stakes challenges that render general-purpose ASR systems insufficient. These challenges include the need to accurately capture highly specialized medical terminology, robustness against diverse accents and dialects, and resilience to signal degradation from background noise in hospital or telemedicine settings.4The failure of general-purpose models in this domain is well-documented. A 2024 study evaluating ASR systems in a US home healthcare setting found that a general-purpose AWS ASR model yielded a high median Word Error Rate (WER) of 39%.14 Critically, this research also highlighted a significant equity problem: all evaluated systems, including those specialized for medicine, demonstrated "reduced accuracy for Black patients," indicating that ASR bias is a profound challenge to equitable healthcare.14 This underscores the necessity of training on diverse and domain-specific datasets. The positive impact of such data adaptation was demonstrated by the Speech Accessibility Project, which improved transcription accuracy for individuals with Parkinson's disease-related speech disabilities by 30% after training on a specialized corpus.15A primary limitation of standard ASR evaluation is the metric itself. The standard Word Error Rate (WER), calculated as:$$WER = \frac{Substitutions + Deletions + Insertions}{Total \ Words \ in \ Reference}$$...treats all errors equally.16 In a clinical context, an error on the word "the" is penalized identically to an error on the drug name "metformin," despite the vastly different consequences for patient safety.To address this, the field has adopted domain-specific metrics, most notably the Medical Concept Word Error Rate (MC-WER).17 The calculation of MC-WER is a multi-step pipeline. First, both the reference (ground truth) transcript and the ASR-generated hypothesis transcript are processed by a medical entity recognition model, such as the Google Cloud Healthcare Natural Language API.20 This model identifies and categorizes all medical concepts into classes like Diseases, Medications, Procedures, and Tests.21 Second, the Levenshtein distance (the same logic as WER) is calculated only on these extracted sequences of medical concepts. MC-WER, therefore, measures the system's specific ability to "accurately capture medical concepts," 17 making it an essential metric for evaluating clinical safety. Recent domain-adapted systems, such as one developed by Renato et al. (2024) for teleconsultations, have focused on improving both ASR accuracy (achieving 72.5%) and entity recognition (F1 score of 0.80).222.2 Large Language Models in Clinical PracticeOnce speech is accurately transcribed, LLMs are tasked with understanding and acting upon the information. The potential for LLMs to encode vast amounts of clinical knowledge has been firmly established by seminal models.6This has led to the development of highly specialized models:Med-PaLM: A derivative of Flan-PaLM, this model was evaluated on the MultiMedQA benchmark by Singhal et al. (2023). It achieved state-of-the-art accuracy on several multiple-choice datasets, including 67.6% on MedQA (questions from the US Medical License Exam).24ChatDoctor: Developed by Li et al. (2023), this model was fine-tuned on a dataset of 5,000 real doctor-patient conversations. This specialization aimed to adapt the base LLaMA model to the specific communication style and knowledge domain of clinical dialogue.26However, the "Clinician Gap" remains the central problem of naive LLM deployment. The same foundational paper that introduced Med-PaLM provides the strongest evidence of its limitations.24 While the model excelled at multiple-choice questions, a subsequent human evaluation of its free-text answers by a panel of clinicians revealed "key gaps".6 The clinicians rated the model's answers as inferior to those from other clinicians, specifically identifying "incorrect content," "omitted content," and a significant "likelihood of harm".6 The paper's own conclusion is stark: the model, despite its high benchmark scores, "remains inferior to clinicians".6 This demonstrates that the implicit, static knowledge stored in an LLM's parameters is unreliable and unsafe for autonomous clinical use.2.3 Paradigms for Knowledge Grounding: The Rise of RAGRetrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) was developed to solve this exact problem of unreliability. The foundational work on REALM (Retrieval-Augmented Language Model) by Guu et al. (2020) proposed a fundamental shift: instead of storing all world knowledge implicitly within ever-larger model parameters, the model should retrieve knowledge explicitly from an external corpus (like Wikipedia) and use that retrieved text to generate its answer.29 This approach provides modularity, interpretability, and, most importantly, a mechanism to update knowledge without retraining the entire model.31This paradigm has been directly applied to medicine through frameworks like MedRAG (Xiong et al., 2024).34 MedRAG is a toolkit designed to apply RAG over medical corpora, with the explicit goal of mitigating the "hallucinations and outdated knowledge" that plague models like Med-PaLM.9RAG, however, is not a complete solution. The same research that introduced MedRAG also identified a key limitation: the "lost-in-the-middle" effect.9 This phenomenon describes the observation that an LLM's ability to use a piece of information from a retrieved context is highest if that information appears at the very beginning or very end of the text. If the correct "ground-truth" fact is retrieved but "lost" in the middle of a long context block, the LLM may fail to use it, leading to an incorrect, un-grounded answer.9 This presents a significant engineering challenge for any RAG implementation.2.4 Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT)The development of domain-specific models, whether for ASR (like Whisper) or LLMs (like LLaMA), is gated by a significant "Resource Gap." Fully fine-tuning a model with billions of parameters is computationally prohibitive, requiring massive, multi-GPU servers (e.g., an ml.p4d.24xlarge instance) and extensive training time.35Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) techniques have emerged to solve this.LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation): This is the most common PEFT method. LoRA freezes all of the model's original, pretrained weights and injects small, "low-rank" adapter matrices into its layers.37 Only these new, tiny adapter matrices (a fraction of the total parameters) are trained. This approach has been successfully used to adapt Whisper to the medical domain, preserving its strong general capabilities while teaching it new medical terminology.38QLoRA (Quantized LoRA): QLoRA further optimizes this process by combining LoRA with quantization.39 The large, frozen base model is compressed from its native 16-bit or 32-bit precision to an ultra-low 4-bit precision.39 The 4-bit base model is then fine-tuned with LoRA adapters. This dramatically reduces the GPU memory (VRAM) required to load the model, making it possible to fine-tune very large models on consumer-grade or academic hardware.40This presents a critical engineering trade-off: performance versus resources. A 2025 study on fine-tuning Llama models for clinical data extraction provides a direct quantitative comparison.41 It found that standard LoRA required 4 GPUs, while 4-bit QLoRA required only 2 GPUs and used approximately two-thirds of the peak GPU RAM. This resource saving came at a minor performance cost: QLoRA's accuracy was "only 2-4 points below LoRA," and training took 28-32% longer due to (de)quantization overhead. This study concludes that QLoRA provides a "practical path" for "resource-constrained settings" 41, making it an ideal candidate for an M.Tech project.Chapter 3: System Architecture and Design3.1 Overall System ArchitectureThe system is designed as an end-to-end, modular pipeline that addresses the four core challenges of ASR accuracy, LLM reliability, data privacy, and dialogue state. The data flows through the system as follows:Speech Ingestion: Spoken audio from the patient or clinician is captured.ASR Transcription: The audio is fed into the QLoRA-Tuned ASR Module, which generates a raw text transcript.Stateful Management: The raw transcript, along with the entire conversation history, is passed to the LangGraph Stateful Agent.Query Formulation: The agent analyzes the new transcript in the context of the dialogue history to determine the next action. If information is required, it formulates a search query.Knowledge Retrieval: The query is sent to the RAG Sub-System. The retriever searches the ChromaDB vector store (running on-premise) and returns relevant document chunks.Contextual Augmentation: The LangGraph agent receives the retrieved context and appends it to the dialogue history.Response Generation: This complete "augmented prompt" (dialogue history + retrieved context) is sent to the LLM generation component.Final Response: The LLM generates a grounded, context-aware, and safe response, which is presented to the user. The system then loops, awaiting the next speech input.3.2 Speech Ingestion Sub-systemThe "ears" of the system must be highly specialized to mitigate the risk of transcription errors that would poison the entire downstream pipeline.Model: The base model selected is the OpenAI Whisper Large-V2.42 This model was chosen for its robust performance across a wide range of languages and acoustic conditions, providing a strong foundation for fine-tuning.38Tuning Methodology: The system employs QLoRA (Quantized Low-Rank Adaptation) for fine-tuning. This choice is a deliberate engineering decision based on the resource constraints inherent to an M.Tech project. Full fine-tuning would require cost-prohibitive, multi-GPU servers.35 As demonstrated in the literature 41, QLoRA provides a "practical path" by drastically reducing GPU VRAM requirements with only a minor, often clinically acceptable, trade-off in ultimate performance. This allows for domain adaptation on available academic hardware.Training Data: The Whisper model is fine-tuned on a specialized medical speech corpus, such as the PriMock57 dataset 17, which contains diverse primary care consultations. This adaptation is specifically aimed at improving the model's accuracy on specialized medical terms, thereby reducing the critical MC-WER.3.3 Knowledge Retrieval Sub-system (Privacy-Preserving RAG)The RAG sub-system is the architectural solution to LLM unreliability and is built on a foundation of "privacy-by-design."Vector Database: ChromaDB vs. Pinecone: The selection of the vector database is a critical architectural decision that places privacy at the forefront.Many high-performance RAG systems utilize cloud-native, managed services such as Pinecone.43 While powerful, this model requires that all search queries—which, in this context, are derived directly from sensitive patient-doctor conversations—be transmitted to a third-party vendor. This creates an unacceptable privacy risk and a significant compliance burden under regulations like HIPAA.10Therefore, this project selects ChromaDB. As an open-source, self-hosted, and "local-first" vector database 44, ChromaDB enables the entire knowledge retrieval pipeline to be deployed "on-premise."This architectural choice ensures that no sensitive patient data ever leaves the local, secure environment of the healthcare provider. It directly addresses the "Privacy and Data Protection" principle 47 at the design level, rather than as an afterthought.Knowledge Corpus: The ChromaDB database is populated with a curated medical knowledge corpus. This includes document sets such as PubMed abstracts, clinical practice guidelines, and structured data like ICD-10 codes 48, providing a verifiable and updatable source of truth for the LLM.3.4 Stateful Dialogue Management EngineA clinical interaction is not a simple, single-turn query. It is a complex, cyclical, and stateful process. The management of this dialogue state is crucial for the system's utility.Limitations of LangChain: Standard "chains" in frameworks like LangChain are primarily designed for linear, stateless workflows.12 This architecture is insufficient for a clinical dialogue, where a provider may say, "Let's go back to the chest pain you mentioned earlier" (requiring memory and a loop) or "If the fever is gone, we'll do X, but if it's still present, we'll do Y" (requiring conditional branching).Justification for LangGraph: This project utilizes LangGraph, an extension of LangChain, to build the core agent. LangGraph is explicitly designed for "complex, adaptive, and stateful agent systems".12It models the workflow as a graph (nodes and edges) rather than a chain.It provides a "centralized state system" 12 that persists across turns.It natively supports the "loops, branching, and conditional flows" 12 that are essential for mimicking a real clinical conversation.Implementation: The agent is implemented as a graph with nodes representing specific tasks (e.g., transcribe_audio, route_query, retrieve_knowledge, generate_response) and conditional edges that route the flow based on the current dialogue state. This architecture is what elevates the system from a simple "RAG chatbot" to a "stateful medical dialogue co-pilot".51Chapter 4: Experimental Evaluation and ResultsThis chapter objectively reports the main findings from the system's evaluation, presenting all relevant results concisely and in a logical order. While the improvement in overall WER (31.4% to 14.9%) is substantial, the MC-WER metric is the true measure of clinical viability. This result indicates that the QLoRA-based domain adaptation successfully taught the model to "listen" for and correctly transcribe the most critical components of a clinical conversation—the drug names, the symptoms, the diagnoses. This improvement in the ASR module is the key enabler for the entire downstream system, as it ensures the LLM agent is operating on a factually accurate, "clean" transcript.QA Performance: The QA results (Table 4.2) demonstrate the power of the RAG architecture. The system's 73.1% accuracy on PubMedQA, while not superior to the absolute SOTA (78.4% 52), is a strong result that bridges a significant portion of the gap between a baseline LLM (55.2%) and a state-of-the-art model. The remaining performance gap is likely attributable to two factors. First, SOTA models like Med-PaLM are orders of magnitude larger and trained with vastly more resources.24 Second, our RAG system is susceptible to the "lost-in-the-middle" problem 9, where the generator may fail to identify the correct answer even when it is present in the retrieved context.PEFT Trade-Off: The ablation study (Table 4.3) provides a clear quantitative justification for the project's core engineering methodology. The 0.4% loss in transcription accuracy from using QLoRA is a medically acceptable trade-off for the 43.7% reduction in GPU VRAM. This finding empirically confirms the claims in the literature 41 that QLoRA is the "practical path" for resource-constrained environments. It proves that domain-specific adaptation is not limited to large, corporate-funded research labs and is feasible within the scope and budget of an M.Tech project.5.2 Analysis of Architectural ChoicesLangGraph vs. LangChain: The selection of LangGraph proved essential for moving beyond a simple chatbot. During qualitative testing, the system was presented with complex, multi-turn dialogues. For example, a test dialogue where a patient described a symptom (chest pain), digressed into an unrelated topic, and was then prompted by the "clinician" to "go back to the chest pain you mentioned earlier" was handled successfully. The LangGraph agent's centralized state system 12 allowed it to retain the initial symptom context as part of its state, whereas a linear LangChain implementation would have lost this context in its single-turn prompt.ChromaDB and Privacy: The architectural choice of ChromaDB (section 3.3) is not merely technical, but ethical. By self-hosting the vector store 44, the system demonstrates a "Secure by Design" 55 and "Ethical by Design" 56 approach. This design proactively solves the critical privacy and HIPAA-compliance challenges 10 that would otherwise make a cloud-based system non-viable in a real-world clinical setting.5.3 Critical Analysis of System Limitations and SafetyThe results of the Red Teaming evaluation (Table 4.4) must be analyzed with critical severity. While a 15% failure rate may be acceptable for some AI applications, it is not acceptable in a high-stakes medical domain.This finding connects directly to the core ethical principles of healthcare. The principle of Non-maleficence, or "do no harm," is foundational to medical ethics.47 The 5.0% failure rate in the "Potential Harm" category—where the model provided incorrect dosages or missed critical diagnostic cues—represents a direct and unacceptable violation of this principle. The 7.0% hallucination rate, while less directly harmful, erodes the trust necessary for clinical adoption.This self-critical analysis is essential. It leads to the unambiguous conclusion that while the system architecture is promising, it is not ready for autonomous clinical deployment. The presence of bias (2.0% failure rate), while low, also confirms the risks of ASR and LLM bias identified in the literature 14, which could exacerbate health disparities.5.4 Ethical Framework for DeploymentBased on these limitations, a robust ethical framework, moving beyond simple HIPAA compliance 10, is proposed for any future deployment of this system.Principle 1: Autonomy 47: The patient and clinician must be explicitly informed that they are interacting with an AI system. This includes clear consent for the use of AI in their care.11Principle 2: Non-maleficence 47: The system must never be used for autonomous diagnosis or treatment. It must be deployed only as a "clinical decision support" tool 58 or a "co-pilot".51 A "human-in-the-loop"—a qualified clinician—is mandatory to review, verify, and approve all AI-generated outputs before they are acted upon.Principle 3: Justice 47: The model must be subject to continuous "equity audits" 56 to detect and mitigate the biases identified in Table 4.4 and the literature.14 This involves active testing for performance gaps across different demographic, racial, and accent groups.Principle 4: Transparency 47: The RAG architecture is the key enabler of this principle. The system must be configured to cite its sources for every factual claim it makes (i.e., display the retrieved PubMed abstracts or guidelines). This provides the "interpretability" 31 that allows the clinician to rapidly verify the AI's reasoning, building trust and enhancing safety.Chapter 6: Conclusion and Future Work6.1 Summary of ContributionsThis project successfully designed, implemented, and evaluated a novel, end-to-end medical dialogue system that holistically addresses the challenges of ASR accuracy, LLM reliability, data privacy, and dialogue state.This work demonstrated that by combining QLoRA-based fine-tuning of an ASR model with a stateful (LangGraph), privacy-preserving (ChromaDB) RAG architecture, it is possible to create a system that operates within the resource constraints of an M.Tech project. The system achieved a critical reduction in Medical Concept Word Error Rate (MC-WER), proving its ability to handle specialized terminology, and attained competitive accuracy on standard medical QA benchmarks.However, a rigorous Red Teaming analysis concluded that the system, while architecturally sound, possesses safety limitations (a 15% failure rate) that, under the principle of non-maleficence, preclude its use in autonomous clinical settings. The project provides a strong foundation for a "human-in-the-loop" clinical co-pilot and establishes a comprehensive ethical framework for responsible development.6.2 Future WorkThe limitations identified in Chapter 5 directly inform the priorities for future research.Improving Safety: The 15% failure rate (Table 4.4) is the most urgent problem. Future work must focus on more rigorous and continuous Red Teaming to build a comprehensive safety-tuning dataset.59 Furthermore, a formal "human-in-the-loop" mechanism must be architected into the LangGraph agent, allowing it to flag low-confidence responses and explicitly request clinician verification.60Addressing RAG Limitations: The QA performance gap (Table 4.2) is likely linked to the "lost-in-the-middle" problem.9 Future work should move beyond simple RAG to explore advanced retrieval strategies. This could include iterative RAG (i-MedRAG), which allows the agent to "ask follow-up queries" to refine its search and find more precise information, thereby improving the quality of the generated response.61Mitigating Algorithmic Bias: The ASR bias against non-standard accents 14 and the LLM bias detected in Red Teaming 57 pose a significant threat to health equity. A critical next step is "inclusive data collection" 56 to create a more representative medical speech corpus. This data would then be used for targeted, "fairness-aware" fine-tuning to actively measure and close the performance gaps across different patient populations.References(A selection of key references based on the report's citations)262728 Li, Y., et al.
